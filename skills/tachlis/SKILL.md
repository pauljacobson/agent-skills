# Weak Brain — re-explain so it lands

## Why this skill exists

There are times in Paul's day when his cognitive bandwidth is low — tired, distracted,
context-switched, or just overloaded — and he still needs to fully understand what
you've been explaining. In those moments your previous answer hasn't failed because it
was wrong; it has failed because it assumed more working memory or more prior context
than Paul currently has available.

The job here is to re-present the same information so it actually lands. Same substance,
clearer scaffolding.

## What this skill is NOT

Be careful not to slip into any of these failure modes — they are condescending, they
waste Paul's time, and they don't help him understand:

- **Not a dumbed-down version.** Don't strip out detail. Don't replace specific terms
  with hand-wavy ones. Paul still wants to make a real decision based on real information.
- **Not a tone shift to "talking to a child".** No "simply put", no "in plain English"
  preamble, no "imagine that…" cute analogies unless an analogy genuinely clarifies.
- **Not a longer version.** Walls of text are the _enemy_ when someone is overloaded.
  Better structure beats more words.
- **Not a rephrase using synonyms.** If the original was confusing, swapping "utilise"
  for "use" doesn't fix it. The fix is structural.
- **Not an apology.** Don't open with "Sorry that was unclear" or "Let me try again".
  Just re-explain.

## What good looks like

The re-explanation should feel like a patient, competent colleague catching Paul up
after he stepped out of the room — assuming he is sharp but doesn't currently have
the prior context loaded.

Aim for these qualities:

1. **Lead with the point.** First sentence: what is this actually about, and what (if
   anything) does Paul need to decide? If there's a question on the table, name it.

2. **Lower the assumed context.** Anything earlier in the conversation that the previous
   explanation depended on — the file you were looking at, the bug you were chasing,
   the option you'd already ruled out — restate briefly. Don't make Paul scroll back.

3. **Unpack jargon inline.** When you use a technical term, define it the first time in
   the same sentence: "the migration (the SQL file that updates the database schema)
   would…". Don't make him hold an undefined term in his head.

4. **One idea per sentence.** Short sentences. Break compound ideas apart. If you find
   yourself writing "and also", that's usually two sentences.

5. **Make structure visible.** Use short headings, numbered steps for sequences, and
   visible separation between "here's the situation" / "here are the options" /
   "here's what I'd recommend" / "here's the question for you". When the brain is
   tired, structure does the work that focused reading would normally do.

6. **Surface options as a real menu.** If there are choices, list them as a small
   numbered list with: what it is (one line), what it costs (one line), what it gets
   you (one line). Don't bury options inside paragraphs.

7. **End with the ask.** Close with a single, clear question or next step — "Do you
   want me to go with option 2?" — so Paul knows exactly what's expected of him.

## How to actually do it

1. **Look back at your most recent substantive message** (or the last few, if the
   conversation has been a back-and-forth on the same topic). That is the material
   you are re-explaining.

2. **Identify the load-bearing pieces:**

   - What was the actual question or topic?
   - What context did the previous explanation assume?
   - Where were the technical terms or compressed ideas?
   - Was there a decision Paul needed to make? What were the options?

3. **Rewrite from scratch, don't edit.** Editing the previous version tends to inherit
   its structural problems. Start with a blank rewrite organised around: situation →
   options (if any) → recommendation (if any) → question for Paul.

4. **Read it back as if you were tired.** Before sending: would this land if you'd
   slept badly and were three meetings deep? If a sentence makes you re-read it, break
   it up.

## Output shape

There's no rigid template — the right shape depends on what you were explaining. But
most re-explanations will fit one of these patterns:

**Pattern A — Explaining a situation or finding:**

```
### What this is about
[One or two sentences naming the topic and why it matters now.]

### The key points
1. [Point one — one short sentence.]
2. [Point two — one short sentence.]
3. [Point three — one short sentence.]

### What I'd suggest
[One paragraph or a single sentence.]

### What I need from you
[The question or next step.]
```

**Pattern B — Presenting options for a decision:**

```
### The decision
[One sentence: what choice is on the table.]

### Background you need
[2–4 short bullets re-establishing context.]

### Your options
1. **[Option name]** — [what it is]. Costs: [tradeoff]. Gets you: [benefit].
2. **[Option name]** — [what it is]. Costs: [tradeoff]. Gets you: [benefit].
3. **[Option name]** — [what it is]. Costs: [tradeoff]. Gets you: [benefit].

### My recommendation
[One short paragraph naming the option and the single most important reason.]

### What I need from you
[The question.]
```

**Pattern C — Walking through a process or sequence:**

```
### What we're doing
[One sentence.]

### The steps
1. [Step — what happens, in plain terms.]
2. [Step — what happens, in plain terms.]
3. [Step — what happens, in plain terms.]

### Where we are now
[One sentence.]

### What's next
[The question or next step.]
```

Pick the pattern that fits. Don't force one if none fits — the principles (lead with
the point, unpack jargon, one idea per sentence, end with the ask) matter more than
the template.

## A quick self-check before sending

- [ ] First sentence names the topic and why it matters now.
- [ ] No undefined jargon.
- [ ] No sentence longer than ~25 words.
- [ ] Structure is visible (headings, lists), not buried in prose.
- [ ] If there's a decision, the options are a clear numbered menu.
- [ ] The message ends with a single clear question or next step.
- [ ] No apology, no "simply put", no condescension.

If any of those fail, fix them before sending.
